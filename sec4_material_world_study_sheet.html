States of matter & phase changes

Solid
Definite shape and volume. Particles vibrate in fixed positions. Incompressible.
Liquid
Definite volume, no fixed shape. Particles slide past each other. Slightly compressible.
Gas
No fixed shape or volume. Particles move rapidly and independently. Highly compressible.
Plasma
Ionized gas with free electrons. Found in stars and lightning. 4th state of matter.

Physical properties

Density
Mass per unit volume. d = m ÷ V (g/cm³ or kg/m³). Intensive property — doesn't change with amount.
Melting/boiling point
Temperature at which a substance changes state. Unique to each pure substance — used for identification.
Solubility
Max amount of solute that dissolves in a solvent at a given temperature. Increases with temp for most solids; decreases for gases.
Electrical conductivity
Ability to conduct electricity. Metals conduct well; ionic compounds conduct when dissolved or melted.

Separation techniques

Filtration
Separates insoluble solid from a liquid using a filter.
Distillation
Separates liquids with different boiling points by evaporation + condensation.
Evaporation
Removes liquid solvent to recover dissolved solid.
Chromatography
Separates substances based on their affinity for stationary vs. mobile phase.
Decantation
Carefully pouring off a liquid to separate it from settled solid or denser liquid.
Centrifugation
Spins mixture at high speed; denser components settle faster to the bottom.

Atomic model

Proton
Positive charge (+1). Found in nucleus. Number = atomic number (Z). Defines the element.
Neutron
No charge. Found in nucleus. Mass ≈ proton. Number = A − Z (mass number − atomic number).
Electron
Negative charge (−1). Orbits in energy levels. Number = protons in neutral atom. Determines bonding.
Nucleus
Dense core containing protons and neutrons. Positively charged overall. Tiny compared to atom's volume.
Key notation: ᴬ₍Z₎X — A = mass number (p⁺ + n⁰), Z = atomic number (p⁺). Example: ¹²₆C = 6 protons, 6 neutrons, 6 electrons.

Periodic table organization

Periods (rows)
Period # = number of electron shells. Going right across a period: atomic number increases, atomic radius decreases, electronegativity increases.
Groups (columns)
Group # = number of valence electrons (for main groups). Elements in same group have similar chemical properties.

Key groups to know

Group 1 — Alkali metals
1 valence electron. Very reactive. React violently with water. Soft metals.
Group 17 — Halogens
7 valence electrons. Highly reactive non-metals. Form −1 ions easily. Example: F, Cl, Br.
Group 18 — Noble gases
Full valence shell. Extremely stable and unreactive. Used as inert atmospheres.
Transition metals
Groups 3–12. Variable oxidation states. Good conductors. Form coloured compounds.

Types of chemical bonds

Ionic bond
Transfer of electrons from metal to non-metal. Creates oppositely charged ions held by electrostatic attraction. High melting point.
Covalent bond
Sharing of electron pairs between non-metals. Can be single, double, or triple bonds. Lower melting point.
Metallic bond
Positive metal ions in a sea of delocalized electrons. Explains conductivity, malleability, and lustre of metals.

Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different A). Example: ¹²C and ¹⁴C are both carbon. Atomic mass on the table is the weighted average of natural isotopes.

Physical vs. chemical changes

Physical change
No new substance formed. Only state, shape, or size changes. Reversible in most cases. Examples: melting, dissolving, cutting.
Chemical change
New substance(s) formed. Original substance is consumed. Usually irreversible. Examples: combustion, rusting, photosynthesis.

Signs of a chemical reaction

Gas production
Bubbles or fizzing observed. Example: acid + carbonate → CO₂.
Precipitate formation
Insoluble solid appears in a solution. Indicates ions have combined.
Colour change
A new substance with different light absorption is produced.
Temperature change
Exothermic (releases heat) or endothermic (absorbs heat) reactions.

Balancing equations — the method

1
Write the unbalanced equation with correct formulas. Never change subscripts — only add coefficients.
2
Count each element on both sides. Start with elements that appear least often (save H and O for last).
3
Add coefficients in front of molecules until both sides have equal atom counts.
4
Verify: same number of each atom on each side. Coefficients must be the smallest whole-number ratio.

Acids and bases (pH)

Acid
Releases H⁺ ions in water. pH < 7. Turns litmus red. Tastes sour. Examples: HCl, H₂SO₄, vinegar.
Base
Releases OH⁻ ions in water. pH > 7. Turns litmus blue. Feels slippery. Examples: NaOH, NH₃.
Neutral
pH = 7. Neither acidic nor basic. Pure water at 25 °C.
Indicators
Substances that change colour with pH. Examples: litmus, phenolphthalein, universal indicator.
pH scale goes 0–14. Each unit = 10× difference in H⁺ concentration. pH 3 is 10× more acidic than pH 4, and 100× more acidic than pH 5.

Law of conservation of mass

Mass of reactants = mass of products. Atoms are neither created nor destroyed — only rearranged. This is why equations must be balanced.

Core concepts

Oxidation
Loss of electrons. Oxidation number increases. The species being oxidized is the reducing agent.
Reduction
Gain of electrons. Oxidation number decreases. The species being reduced is the oxidizing agent.
Redox reaction
Simultaneous oxidation and reduction. Electrons are transferred from one species to another.
Electrolyte
Substance that conducts electricity when dissolved (ions carry the charge). Examples: NaCl(aq), H₂SO₄(aq).
Memory trick: OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).

Activity series (metals)

The activity series ranks metals by their tendency to lose electrons (be oxidized). A more active metal displaces a less active one from solution. Example: Zn is above Cu, so Zn displaces Cu²⁺ from solution.

Most active → least active (simplified): Li, K, Ca, Na, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Ni, Sn, Pb, H, Cu, Ag, Au

Corrosion & protection

Corrosion
Oxidation of a metal by its environment (water, oxygen). Iron rusting = formation of Fe₂O₃·xH₂O.
Cathodic protection
Attach a more active metal (sacrificial anode) to the metal you want to protect. The active metal oxidizes instead.
Electroplating
Uses electrolysis to coat an object with a thin metal layer. Object = cathode. Plating metal = anode.

What is organic chemistry?

Organic chemistry is the study of carbon-containing compounds. Carbon forms 4 bonds, allowing chains, branches, and rings. Carbon backbones can be saturated (only C–C single bonds) or unsaturated (contain C=C or C≡C).

Naming alkanes (IUPAC)

1
Find the longest continuous carbon chain. This gives the parent name: meth(1), eth(2), prop(3), but(4), pent(5), hex(6), hept(7), oct(8).
2
Number the chain from the end closest to any branch or functional group.
3
Name each branch as an alkyl group (methyl, ethyl, propyl…) with its position number.
4
List substituents alphabetically. Add suffix: -ane (alkane), -ene (alkene), -yne (alkyne), -ol (alcohol).

Isomers

Structural isomers
Same molecular formula, different connectivity. Example: butane (C₄H₁₀) and 2-methylpropane both have formula C₄H₁₀ but different structures.
Stereoisomers
Same connectivity, different spatial arrangement. Less commonly tested at Sec 4 ST level. Cis/trans isomers around a double bond are the main example.

Key organic reactions

Combustion
Organic compound + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O (complete) or CO + H₂O (incomplete). Releases energy.
Addition
Two reactants combine to form one product across a double bond. Example: alkene + H₂ → alkane (hydrogenation).
Substitution
One atom or group replaces another. Common in alkanes with halogens under UV light.
Esterification
Carboxylic acid + alcohol → ester + water. Used to make perfumes, flavors, and biodiesel.
Polymerization
Monomers link to form a long chain polymer. Addition polymerization (alkenes) or condensation polymerization (loses H₂O).

Petroleum & fossil fuels

Petroleum is a mixture of hydrocarbons separated by fractional distillation. Each fraction has a different boiling point range and chain length. Shorter chains = lower boiling point, more flammable. Examples: natural gas (C₁–C₄), gasoline (C₅–C₁₂), diesel (C₁₂–C₂₀), heavy fuel oil (C₂₀+).
